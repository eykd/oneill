# Weather in an O'Neill cylinder

This answers worldbuilding.stackexchange question 159233, "Hurricane-force winds or gentle breezes in an O'Neill cylinder?" The habitat is the question's: radius R = 3.2 km, length L = 20 km, spinning at 0.5286 rpm for 1 g at the floor (period 113.5 s, rim speed 177 m/s), with three window strips between three land strips of low buildings and trees.

Tables are generated by this package's models (`oneill atmosphere`). Literature values come from `docs/research/atmosphere-literature.md`, cited by question number (Q1 to Q12), where each carries a confidence tag.

## TL;DR

**Provisional.** The 2D weather runs are still going, and the part of the answer that depends on them is marked.

Once the air co-rotates with the hull, the habitat has no built-in wind. The weather is whatever the sunlight through the windows drives: Earth-like convection, with thermals over the land strips and a cloud base about 1.8 km up (O'Neill air, 290 K, 50 % humidity). <!-- PROVISIONAL res-check --> The 2D simulation puts the breeze at 10 m at about 1 m/s (2 to 2.5 mph), averaged over the floor and over day and night, with spatial peaks near 4 m/s (9 mph). The 3 m/s in the surface tables is a separate assumed input for studying gusts and stability.

The "blender" exists only while the hull changes speed. Spinning up from rest in an hour leaves winds near 18 m/s at 10 m above a suburban canopy-drag floor and about a day of catch-up. A 24-hour spin-up cuts that to roughly a third.

Rotation still leaves fingerprints. Rising air is pushed spinward (the question had that sign backward), though thermals stretched along the axis absorb most of the push, and spinward wind near the rooftops should be gustier than antispinward wind. The Coriolis force is about 760 times Earth's, strong enough to push slow flow toward two dimensions, where eddies can grow large instead of staying small.

In the 2D model the strip heating neither locks the flow to the three land strips nor builds habitat-scale vortices (hypothesis H1). Eddies settle near the forcing scale, about 1 to 2 km (0.7 to 1.2 miles), and there is no prevailing wind. That holds when heat leaves the air through the floor strips. If the air sheds heat everywhere instead, the flow condenses into vortices the size of the habitat, so where the heat goes is a design choice that shapes the weather.

## The question

The atmosphere the question describes (½ atm, 20 % O₂, 30 % N₂) is not O'Neill's. His 1974 *Physics Today* paper specifies "the air composition as well as the atmospheric pressure are those of sea level on Earth." The half-atmosphere comes from NASA SP-413, the 1975 Stanford torus study, at 50.8 kPa total (Q1). The package carries both as presets, "O'Neill 1974" and "SP-413", and every table reports both.

The question says anything moving radially is deflected antispinward. That is half right: falling things land antispinward, but rising things are pushed spinward. Some answers put Coriolis at "1200×" or "thousands of times" Earth's. By rotation rate it is 759× (numbers table), and the period is 113.5 s, not the half-minute one answer gives.

## Co-rotation is the default state

Air at rest relative to the hull feels centrifugal gravity toward the floor and a pressure gradient pushing back, and they balance when pressure rises toward the rim as the next section describes. Coriolis acts only on air moving relative to the hull, so still air feels none. Solid-body rotation is also stable by Rayleigh's criterion: angular momentum per unit mass rises outward, so a displaced ring of air returns to where it started (Q4).

So in a sealed hull wind needs a driver, either uneven heating or a change in the spin rate. The rest of this document is about those two.

One apparent third driver cancels. Warm air is lighter, so it carries less Coriolis force than cold air, and an early version of the 2D model turned that difference into a steady retrograde breeze of 2 to 3 m/s. That was an error. Coriolis acts on the mass flux, and mass is conserved in a sealed hull, so the net torque on every ring of air vanishes once temperatures stop changing (`docs/design/annulus2d.md` §1). The effect moves angular momentum around while the air warms or cools and gives no steady mean wind.

## Pressure and temperature from rim to axis

The natural height coordinate is centrifugal geopotential, Φ = Ω²(R² − r²)/2, the energy per kilogram to lift something from the floor to radius r. It plays the role of g·z, so every Earth formula for pressure, lapse rate and cloud base carries over exactly when written in Φ (`equilibrium.py`). Floor to axis is 15.69 kJ/kg, the same as Earth's lowest 1.6 km. The habitat's whole sky is as deep, thermodynamically, as Earth's boundary layer.

Axis pressure is about 82 % of floor pressure for either gas, isothermal or adiabatic (0.828 against 0.824 for O'Neill air at 290 K). O'Neill's own profile gives 0.825 (Q1). Cloud base is where true height and Earth-equivalent height part ways. For O'Neill air at 290 K and 50 % humidity, condensation starts at the geopotential Earth reaches at 1306 m, which here is 1828 m up, more than halfway to the axis. At 70 % humidity it drops to 787 m. O'Neill quoted "between 1100 and 1400 meters" using Earth lapse rates, so those are Earth-equivalent heights. Converted through Φ they become 1.4 to 2.1 km (Q1), matching the table.

## Convection, thermals and clouds

Sunlit land heats the air above it, typically 100 to 350 W/m² by day over Earth-like land (Q10). At ½ atm, with 200 W/m² and a 1 km mixed layer, Deardorff's scale gives updrafts near 2.2 m/s (Q10). The convective Rossby number is about 0.02, so rotation shapes every thermal.

The parcel model (`parcel.py`) releases one warm parcel at the floor into still air, with buoyancy, Coriolis and an optional linear drag.

The drag-free rows show rotation at its most extreme. Rising pushes the parcel spinward, and spinward motion makes it heavier, so it loops in an inertial circle with a 57 s period. A 1 K parcel never rises above 5.5 m. The trap height is b/(2Ω²), about 5.5 m per kelvin of excess in O'Neill air, and angular momentum fixes the drift of a free parcel at about 11.3 m/s per 100 m of rise (claim (a) in the research summary).

Both numbers are ceilings from a model with no pressure response. A 100 m thermal at Rossby number 0.01 is deep in the Taylor–Proudman regime, and inertial waves stretch it along the axis within about one inertial period. For a column uniform along the axis the Coriolis force is a pure gradient, its own pressure perturbation absorbs it, and it gets no net spinward push (Q7, Q8). Compact 3D thermals fall between the parcel and the column.

Drag releases even the parcel. With drag equal to 2Ω, a 3 K parcel climbs steadily at 0.46 m/s and reaches cloud base (50 % humidity) in 99 minutes; a 1 K parcel takes 4.9 hours. Thermals do reach cloud base, at a few tenths of a m/s against the 2.2 m/s convective scale, leaning spinward by at most the tabulated drift. The parcel is dry, so cloud latent heat is not counted.

Spinning-annulus experiments agree: centrifugal convection with a hot outer wall forms axis-parallel rolls and goes quasi-2D once the inverse Rossby number passes 10 to 15 (Jiang et al. 2020; Q7). The habitat sits near 19 for 1 K over 1 km.

## Coriolis: what the residents feel

The fictitious-force half of the package (`oneill`, no subcommand) gives the human-scale numbers. In the floor frame (x spinward, y axial, z up toward the axis) the Coriolis acceleration is 2Ω(w x̂ − u ẑ), with 2Ω = 0.1107 s⁻¹, or 1.13 % of floor gravity per m/s. Rising motion is pushed spinward and spinward motion toward the floor. Axial motion is parallel to the spin and feels nothing (research doc, "Sign convention").

Walking, Coriolis is purely vertical. A 1.4 m/s stroll spinward makes you 1.6 % heavier (101.587 % of g), and antispinward 1.6 % lighter (98.426 %). At 3 m/s the change is 3.4 %. Walking along the axis changes nothing, and nobody is pushed sideways on level ground.

Climbing brings the sideways push: 1.13 % of g spinward per m/s, 7.8 N for a 70 kg person on a 1 m/s ladder, antispinward going down. The push keeps its size with height while local gravity falls (to 50 % halfway up), so near the axis the apparent vertical tilts strongly.

Dropped objects land antispinward of the point below: 0.53 m from 10 m, 17 m from a 100 m tower. A vertical jump lands slightly spinward. So the question's "deflected antispinward" holds for falling things and is backward for rising ones. Wind follows the same rules, and spinward wind presses harder on the ground than antispinward wind (the Eötvös effect).

At that wind, suburban gusts reach 5.5 m/s, a gust factor of 1.83, in line with the 1.7 to 2.0 typical over suburbs and forests on Earth (Q9). The ½ atm air carries half the dynamic pressure, so wind loads halve (gusts of 18.3 Pa against 9.3 Pa over the suburb). The forest's 7.5 m/s gusts are overestimates, because the 20 m reference height sits inside its roughness sublayer.

Rotation changes which shear is turbulent. Shear whose vorticity opposes the spin is destabilised, and shear whose vorticity follows it is damped, as in spanwise-rotating channels (Brethouwer 2017; Q5). On the floor, spinward wind opposes the spin and is unstable only where its shear exceeds 2Ω. Antispinward wind is stable at every shear, and axial wind is neutral.

Above canopy top the log-law shear soon drops below 2Ω, and the area-averaged canopy profile is gentle too, so the unstable layer is a slab near canopy top. At the assumed 3 m/s it spans 4.6 to 13 m over the suburb, 2.6 to 12.3 m over the orchard and 8.7 to 24.3 m over the forest. A plain log law put it in the bottom 1 to 7 m (Q5); the canopy lifts it. This is an area-averaged result, and the much sharper shear around individual buildings is not represented. The upshot is that the same wind should be gustier near rooftops when it blows spinward. How much gustier has never been measured for this geometry.

## Ekman spirals and boundary layers

There is no classical Ekman spiral on the floor. An Ekman layer needs part of the spin perpendicular to the wall, and the floor is parallel to the spin, like Earth's ground at the equator. Axial wind feels no Coriolis force, and spinward or antispinward wind feels only a wall-normal one that pressure absorbs, so nothing turns the wind in the floor's plane (claim (b), Q3).

The end caps, with the spin normal to them, carry true Ekman layers with spirals. They are thin, 1.6 cm with molecular viscosity and 4 to 13 m with eddy viscosities of 1 to 10 m²/s (numbers table), and always turbulent: at molecular viscosity they go unstable above about 0.16 m/s of wind (Q2).

The floor instead hosts Stewartson-type layers (Stewartson 1957; Q3). Laminar theory gives an outer layer of thickness H·E^¼, 200 to 370 m with eddy viscosity, carrying the jump in azimuthal wind, and an inner layer of H·E^⅓, 60 to 120 m, carrying the axial return flow (numbers table). At habitat Reynolds numbers every layer is turbulent, so these scalings give structure only (Q3).

The floor's nearest thing to a spiral is a turbulent veer. Channel simulations rotating about the streamwise axis, the axial-wind case here, show a near-wall cross-flow veering 2° to 11° at a few percent of the wind speed (Oberlack et al. 2006; Q6). The direction for the habitat is unverified. This is hypothesis H2.

## Von Kármán vortex streets

Vortex streets need wind relative to an obstacle, so they appear only in thermally driven breezes. For a 10 m building at 3 m/s and Strouhal number 0.2, shedding runs at 0.060 Hz, 3.4 times 2Ω/2π (numbers table). Rectangular buildings shed nearer St ≈ 0.12, giving 0.036 Hz (Okajima 1982; Q11). Either way the 17 to 28 s shedding period is comparable to the 57 s inertial period.

With the spin perpendicular to the obstacle axis, as for a building, towed-cylinder experiments find rotation keeps the wake steady only up to a Reynolds number near (275 ± 25)/Ro, with Ro = U/(2Ωd) (Machicoane et al. 2018; Q11). A 10 m building at 2 to 5 m/s has Ro of 1.8 to 4.5 and a Reynolds number near 10⁶, far past that, so shedding persists, with tilted wakes radiating inertial waves. Obstacles of 50 m or more have Ro below 1 and are rotation-controlled. Nobody has measured Strouhal numbers for this orientation.

Flow over ridges in a spanwise-rotating frame separates less on the anticyclonic side and more on the cyclonic side (Savino & Wu 2024; Q11), so spinward and antispinward winds over rows of buildings should leave different lee wakes. That is untested for habitat geometry.

Viscosity is larger where the air's angular momentum falls outward (Rayleigh-unstable) and smaller elsewhere (Q4).

With end caps and the suburban canopy-drag floor, spinning from rest in one hour puts the wind at 10 m near 18 to 23 m/s, with 7 to 23 hours to reach 90 % co-rotation depending on eddy viscosity. A 24-hour ramp drops the 10 m wind to 5 to 6 m/s. The "peak wind anywhere" column is mostly air near the axis that has not started turning; residents feel the 10 m column. These fast-ramp figures are order-of-magnitude only, because the end-cap model is the linear Greenspan–Howard result, valid for ε = |u|/(Ωr) ≤ 0.3, and every spin-up from rest exceeds it.

The end caps run the clock. Without them the same spin-ups take 38 hours to 15 days. With them, τ_E = (L/2)/√(νΩ) sets the pace, 11.8 h at ν = 1 m²/s and 3.7 h at 10 m²/s (numbers table), so adjustment times scale with the square root of the new spin rate.

I expected spin-down to be much faster than spin-up. It is not. When the hull slows, the air beside the floor outruns it, which is centrifugally unstable (Kaiser et al. 2020; Q4), while spin-up is stable. But the unstable layer is thin and the end caps set the clock: a 10 % slowdown adjusts in 23.8 h and a 10 % speedup in 22.0 h. Spin-down is the slower of the pair because the end-cap sink scales with the new, lower rate.

Slow changes are gentle. A 1 % per day change leaves a lag wind of 0.37 m/s at 10 m (ν = 1 m²/s, end caps on). Molecular viscosity alone would take most of a year, but at these Reynolds numbers the laminar picture does not apply.

## The 2D weather simulation

The L3 model (`annulus2d.py`) solves the Boussinesq equations in the habitat's (r, θ) cross-section, assuming no variation along the axis. Strong rotation pushes slow flow toward exactly that (Taylor–Proudman), and in strict 2D Coriolis drops out of the vorticity equation (Q8). Two-dimensional turbulence then moves energy to larger scales, an inverse cascade, until friction stops it. Hypothesis H1 is that this happens here.

Gravity is centrifugal, so warm air is pushed toward the axis and azimuthal temperature differences make vorticity. Land strips take 200 W/m² by day and −30 W/m² by night (Q10). In the primary run the windows are the matching sink, so net heating is zero at every moment. Eddy viscosity is 1 m²/s (10 in a sensitivity run), the floor is L1's canopy-drag layer, and the end caps damp the vorticity. An inner wall at 0.1 R is a numerical artifact, so runs at 0.05 R and 0.2 R test it.

The friction that arrests a cascade here is the end caps, which act on every axis-parallel column; the canopy is a thin ring at the disc's edge. The predicted arrest wavelength is 0.08 to 0.18 times √(ε/μ³) (Boffetta 2007; Q8): about 0.5 to 1 km if the cascade carries 10⁻⁶ W/kg, 5 to 11 km at 10⁻⁴ W/kg (`docs/design/annulus2d.md` §6). The run measures ε itself.

Each validation test is built to fail if its ingredient is wrong (`docs/design/annulus2d.md` §5). A Lamb–Oseen vortex decays at the analytic rate, heating with no azimuthal variation makes no flow, angular momentum closes against wall and end-cap torques, and thin-gap onset matches the Rayleigh–Bénard 1708 within 3 % (fixed-flux 720, Hurle, Jakeman & Pike 1967).

**Results.** Within 2D, the answer to H1 is neither. In the flux-neutral cases, where heat enters and leaves the air through the floor strips, the flow does not lock to the three-strip pattern and does not condense into habitat-scale vortices. It sits near the forcing scale. The m = 3 peak of the azimuthal spectrum is broadband, the energy fraction locked to the strips is 0.18 to 0.35, and modes m ≤ 2 hold a 0.11 to 0.20 share. The energy-weighted eddy size is about 1.1 to 1.9 km (0.7 to 1.2 miles).

The steady-heating case shows that the night is not what keeps eddies small. It ran 10 days with constant heating and no nightly reset, and modes m = 1 and 2 still never grew. The flow is arrested near the forcing scale.

Where the heat leaves the air changes the answer. In the volumetric-sink case the air loses heat everywhere and the windows are inert, and the flow turns condensate-like: the spectrum peaks at m = 2, m ≤ 2 holds 0.65 of the energy, total energy is about 45 times higher, and domain-scale vortices are visible. How a real habitat sheds its heat is a design choice, and on this evidence it decides between kilometre-scale eddies and habitat-scale ones.

The other knobs barely matter. Switching Coriolis-buoyancy on or off changes nothing, and neither does moving the inner wall anywhere from 0.05 R to 0.2 R. Raising the eddy viscosity to 10 m²/s lifts the lock fraction to 0.35 and leaves the winds unchanged.

There is no prevailing wind. The zonal-mean wind, averaged around the habitat, is under 0.02 m/s with Coriolis-buoyancy on or off, which fits the withdrawal of the retrograde breeze described earlier.

## What about Navier–Stokes?

Does the unsolved mathematics undermine a model like this? As of October 2026: *a forced Navier–Stokes blowup has been claimed (OpenAI, unrefereed) and Clay says the problem has "apparently been settled". The unforced question remains open. None of this affects engineering turbulence models, which never relied on it* (Q12).

Fefferman's official Clay statement accepts any of four answers: smoothness with zero force on R³ or the torus, (A) and (B), or breakdown with a smooth decaying force, (C) and (D). On 8 September 2026 OpenAI posted a 166-page manuscript, "Finite Time Blowup for Navier–Stokes", claiming (C) and (D). It is not on arXiv and has no journal record. The Clay Institute's notice of 11 September contemplates "the announcement that the Navier-Stokes problem has apparently been settled" and adds "The process is deliberately unhurried". It names no author and awards nothing, its rules require publication, two years and "general acceptance", and the problem page still reads "Active" (Q12).

What is established is narrower. Chen and Hou proved, with computer-assisted estimates, smooth-data blowup for 3D axisymmetric Euler in a domain with a solid boundary (Part II in *Multiscale Model. Simul.* 2025, reviewed in *PNAS* 2025). That is inviscid and bounded, so not prize-eligible. Early preprints on the OpenAI claim constrain it without refuting it (Constantin, Ignatova & Vicol), dispute that its Lean formalisation matches the written proof (Bastounis, Circelli & Hansen), and note its mechanism needs similarity radii of order 10²⁰: "A real fluid cavitates (water) or shocks (air) first" (Duraiswami). The unforced problem is open (Q12).

For habitat weather nothing changes. The known singularities are engineered, and turbulence models describe averaged statistics at finite Reynolds number, calibrated against experiment, and never assumed global smoothness. Any blowup would sit far below this flow's 1 to 2 mm Kolmogorov scale, and these models use cells of metres to kilometres (Q12).

## Open questions

**H1: big eddies or locked cells.** Does axis-invariant convection from three land strips build kilometre-scale eddies, or settle into cells locked to the strips? Nothing in the literature treats three-fold heating (Q7). Within 2D, L3 answers neither, and the outcome hinges on where heat leaves the air. What happens once the flow varies along the axis is still open.

**H2: the axial-wind veer.** Axial wind near the floor should veer a few degrees, the floor's stand-in for an Ekman spiral (Q6). Its direction here is unverified, and the size comes from a simulation at Re_τ = 180.

**H3: the floor's boundary layer.** Laminar theory gives Stewartson layers on a wall parallel to the spin, but the theory for a driving sidewall is incomplete and every layer here is turbulent (Q3). Whether floor drag or end-cap drag dominates the long-term momentum budget is open too; the floor has six times the end caps' area (Q2).

Some inputs could not be verified (research doc, "Could not verify"). There is no peer-reviewed turbulent spin-up time, only our quadratic-drag derivation, and the spin-down eddy viscosity is extrapolated from simulations six orders of magnitude smaller in Reynolds number. Nobody has published a dynamical study of a rotating-habitat atmosphere (claim (g)).

## Reproducing these numbers

From the repository root:

```sh
uv run oneill atmosphere --write-doc docs/atmosphere.md
```

This rewrites only the generated blocks (each fenced by a pair of HTML comment markers) and leaves the prose alone; a test fails if the committed file is stale. Without `--write-doc` it prints the blocks, and `--figures DIR` saves the figures as PNG.

The fictitious-force numbers in the Coriolis section come from:

```sh
uv run oneill --sections lateral vertical ballistics
```

The L3 cases run one at a time and merge into `docs/data/l3_summary.json`, which the `annulus` block reads:

```sh
uv run oneill atmosphere --run-l3 OUT_DIR --l3-case primary --l3-days DAYS
uv run oneill atmosphere --write-doc docs/atmosphere.md
```

The cases are `primary`, `cb_off`, `nu10`, `rin005`, `rin02`, `steady` and `uniform_sink`.
